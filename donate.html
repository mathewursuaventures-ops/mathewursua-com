Why donate

Comeback of the Century is a recovery campaign, not a luxury appeal.

Comeback of the Century presents Mathew F. Ursua’s memoir and social commentary on obesity, education, family formation, psychiatric treatment, social mobility, and the search for answers. The flyer frames the central question bluntly: why did marriage, wealth, and social success remain so difficult when the public explanation may be incomplete?

The donation argument is that Ursua needs concentrated time and resources to continue rebuilding the body, mind, career platform, and long-term economic structure that were damaged by years of obesity, debt, social isolation, psychiatric treatment, and delayed development. The request is for supporters to help create the conditions in which recovery can become durable rather than temporary.

The practical thesis: a full-time wage schedule can interfere with the constant physical training, recovery, writing, and asset-building needed to complete this phase of the comeback.

The flyer specifically asks supporters to contribute toward the Take Title℠ movement, including cash gifts and, where legally and practically appropriate, high-equity property or other assets that could support future real-estate participation. The long-term vision is to trade toward income-producing properties such as apartment buildings and strip malls in stronger mainland markets, where rents may support positive cash flow and long-term independence.

Body first

Fitness is the infrastructure.

Ursua’s claim is that his recovery depends on intensive bodybuilding, long walks with dumbbells, cardio, and preparation for the Los Angeles Marathon cycle leading toward the 2028 Olympic era. In this framing, training is not recreation. It is a survival system.

He reports from firsthand experience that hard exercise, heat, soreness, bruising, muscular work, and sustained cardio help keep him mentally well in a way he believes medication did not. This is presented as personal experience and public commentary, not medical advice.

Work and recovery

Wage labor is not neutral when the body is still being rebuilt.

The flyer argues that Ursua has relied on significant pain management to perform low-wage cleaning work while still trying to train. The campaign position is that if so much physical pain is being absorbed for so little economic gain, the better use of time may be a focused recovery schedule: training, writing, reporting, organizing records, and building the platform.

The economic argument is simple: recovery requires hours, food, equipment, transportation, rest, and consistency. A conventional work schedule can consume the very time and strength needed to escape the trap.

The deeper argument

Psychiatry, autism-style traits, stimulation, and the road not taken.

The campaign argues that awkwardness, autism-style traits, delayed social development, and difficulty with conversation should not automatically be interpreted through a narrow clinical lens. Ursua’s own interpretation is that earlier exposure to high-stimulation environments, physical stress, flirtation, pressure, crowds, noise, challenge, and real social feedback might have trained his nervous system better than isolation, overprotection, or medication.

In this account, adrenaline and fight-or-flight energy are not treated only as symptoms to suppress. Ursua frames them as forces that can drive exercise, action, social learning, and self-correction. He argues that suppressing that energy can remove the very impulse that makes him walk, lift, train, and push through fear.

The page does not ask readers to accept every interpretation as settled fact. It asks them to recognize the central public-interest question: what happens when a person’s development is medically, socially, economically, and institutionally redirected before the person has had the chance to build strength, confidence, sexuality, economic power, and normal adult capacity?
